Double Jeopardy

Overview and the Three Protections When Jeopardy Attaches The "Same Offense" — Blockburger Dual (Separate) Sovereignty Permissible Retrials

Overview and the Three Protections

The Fifth Amendment's Double Jeopardy Clause provides that no person shall "be subject for the same offence to be twice put in jeopardy of life or limb." The Clause is applicable to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment. It safeguards a defendant's interest in the finality of a criminal proceeding and against the ordeal of repeated prosecutions for the same conduct.

The Three Guarantees of the Double Jeopardy Clause

The Clause protects against:

  1. A second prosecution for the same offense after ACQUITTAL;
  2. A second prosecution for the same offense after CONVICTION; and
  3. MULTIPLE PUNISHMENTS for the same offense.

The Clause does NOT bar a subsequent CIVIL action arising from the same conduct. A criminal defendant who is acquitted may still face civil liability (e.g., a wrongful death suit) for the same underlying acts, because the civil action is not a criminal "jeopardy."

Because double jeopardy is a highly tested bar subject, the analysis proceeds in a predictable sequence: (1) determine whether jeopardy attached in the first proceeding; (2) determine whether the two charges are the "same offense" under the Blockburger test; (3) ask whether the two prosecutions are brought by separate sovereigns; and (4) if the same sovereign is re-prosecuting, ask whether a recognized exception permits the retrial.

When Jeopardy Attaches

Double jeopardy protections apply only once jeopardy has "attached." The moment of attachment marks the point at which the defendant is first placed at risk of conviction. If a case is dismissed before jeopardy attaches, there is no double-jeopardy bar to bringing the charges again.

Rule — Moment of Attachment

  • Jury trial: Jeopardy attaches when the jury is empaneled and sworn.
  • Bench trial: Jeopardy attaches when the first witness is sworn.
  • Guilty plea: Jeopardy attaches when the court accepts the plea.

Consequence: A dismissal, nolle prosequi, or discharge occurring before these points does not bar a later prosecution, because the defendant was never actually placed in jeopardy.

Type of Proceeding Point of Attachment Dismissal Before This Point
Jury trial Jury empaneled and sworn No bar — may reprosecute
Bench trial First witness sworn No bar — may reprosecute
Guilty plea Court accepts the plea No bar — may refile

The "Same Offense" — The Blockburger Test

The Double Jeopardy Clause bars successive prosecution only for the "same offense." Two crimes arising from the same act or transaction are analyzed under the "same elements" test of Blockburger v. United States, 284 U.S. 299 (1932).

The Blockburger "Same Elements" Test

Two offenses are the "same offense" unless each requires proof of an element the other does not.

  • If each offense contains at least one distinct element that the other lacks, they are different offenses, and successive prosecution (or cumulative punishment) is permitted.
  • If one offense's elements are entirely contained within the other (so that one does not have a distinct element), they are the same offense, and the second prosecution is barred.

Lesser-Included Offenses

A lesser-included offense is one whose elements are a subset of the greater offense's elements — the lesser has no distinct element of its own. Under Brown v. Ohio:

  • Conviction or acquittal of the greater offense bars a later prosecution for a lesser included offense; and
  • Conviction or acquittal of the lesser offense bars a later prosecution for the greater offense.

Exception (not tested here as a limit but worth noting): a later prosecution for the greater offense may be permitted where a necessary element (e.g., the victim's death) had not yet occurred at the time of the first prosecution.

Blockburger v. United States
284 U.S. 299 (1932)

Where the same act or transaction constitutes a violation of two distinct statutory provisions, the test to determine whether there are two offenses or only one is whether each provision requires proof of a fact which the other does not.

Facts

Blockburger was convicted of multiple violations of the Harrison Narcotic Act arising from sales of morphine to the same purchaser. He challenged his cumulative sentences on two counts based on a single sale, arguing that he was being punished twice for the same offense.

Reasoning

The Court articulated what became the definitive "same elements" test. Two statutory provisions define the "same offense" only if neither requires proof of a fact that the other does not. Because one count required proof that the sale was not made from the original stamped package and the other required proof that the sale was not made pursuant to a written order, each demanded proof of a distinct element. The offenses were therefore separate, and cumulative punishment did not violate double jeopardy.

Blockburger supplies the controlling constitutional definition of "same offense" for both the multiple-punishment and successive-prosecution branches of the Double Jeopardy Clause. If each offense has at least one distinct element, they are different offenses.

Dual (Separate) Sovereignty

The Double Jeopardy Clause bars only a second prosecution by the same sovereign. Under the dual sovereignty doctrine, successive prosecutions for the same conduct brought by separate sovereigns are not barred, because an act denounced as a crime by two sovereigns is an offense against each.

Who Counts as a Separate Sovereign

  • Federal government and a State are separate sovereigns (Gamble v. United States, 2019).
  • Two different States are separate sovereigns (Heath v. Alabama).
  • A municipality and its own State are the SAME sovereign (a city derives its power from the state), so successive prosecutions by a city and its state are barred.
Two Prosecuting Entities Same or Separate Sovereign? Successive Prosecution?
United States and a State Separate Permitted
State A and State B Separate Permitted
City and its own State Same Barred
Gamble v. United States
587 U.S. 678 (2019)

The dual sovereignty doctrine remains valid: because the federal government and a state are separate sovereigns, a defendant may be prosecuted by both for the same conduct without violating the Double Jeopardy Clause.

Facts

Terance Gamble was convicted in Alabama state court of possession of a firearm by a convicted felon. The federal government then prosecuted him under a federal felon-in-possession statute for the very same instance of possession. Gamble moved to dismiss the federal charge, urging the Court to overrule the dual sovereignty doctrine.

Reasoning

The Court declined to overrule the doctrine, reaffirming a rule with 170 years of precedent behind it. The word "offence" in the Clause refers to a violation of a sovereign's law, and where two sovereigns each have authority to define crimes, a single act constitutes two distinct offenses. The States and the federal government draw their power to punish from separate and independent sources, so a prosecution by one does not bar a prosecution by the other. Principles of stare decisis further counseled against disturbing the settled rule.

Confirms that a defendant may face both federal and state prosecution for the same act. Contrast the municipality-and-state relationship, which involves a single sovereign and therefore falls within the Clause's bar.

Permissible Retrials — When Jeopardy Does Not Bar a Second Trial

Even after jeopardy has attached, several circumstances permit the government to try the defendant again. The unifying theme is that these situations do not implicate the finality interest the Clause protects — the first proceeding produced no valid acquittal on the merits.

Retrial Is Permitted After:

  • A hung jury / mistrial for "manifest necessity." When a jury cannot reach a verdict, or a mistrial is declared out of manifest necessity (e.g., a deadlocked jury, a fundamentally prejudicial error), the defendant may be retried.
  • A mistrial granted at the DEFENDANT'S request. A defendant who successfully moves for a mistrial generally may be retried — unless the prosecutor goaded the defendant into requesting the mistrial through intentional misconduct designed to provoke it (Oregon v. Kennedy).
  • A defendant's SUCCESSFUL APPEAL. A defendant who obtains reversal of a conviction on appeal may ordinarily be retried, because the reversal wipes the slate clean.

Two Critical Limits on Retrial After Appeal

  • Insufficient evidence bars retrial. If a conviction is reversed because the evidence was legally insufficient to support it, retrial is barred — a reversal for insufficiency is the equivalent of an acquittal (Burks v. United States). This differs from reversal for trial error (e.g., improperly admitted evidence), after which retrial is allowed.
  • Implied acquittal. A defendant cannot be retried for an offense of which he was impliedly acquitted. For example, if a jury convicts of a lesser offense but is silent on the greater charge, the silence operates as an acquittal of the greater offense, barring its retrial.

Burks v. United States
437 U.S. 1 (1978)

The Double Jeopardy Clause bars retrial once a reviewing court has found the evidence at the first trial legally insufficient to sustain the conviction; a reversal for evidentiary insufficiency is functionally an acquittal.

Facts

Burks was convicted of bank robbery after raising an insanity defense. The Court of Appeals reversed, holding that the prosecution had failed to carry its burden of proving sanity — that is, the evidence was insufficient to support the verdict — and remanded to consider whether to order a new trial.

Reasoning

The Court distinguished reversals for trial error from reversals for insufficient evidence. Reversal for trial error implies nothing about guilt or innocence and simply means the defendant was convicted through a defective process, so retrial is permissible. A reversal for insufficiency, by contrast, means the government failed to prove its case and the defendant was entitled to a judgment of acquittal. Allowing the prosecution a second opportunity to supply evidence it failed to muster the first time would be precisely the abuse the Clause forbids.

Draws the pivotal line for retrial after a successful appeal: trial-error reversals permit retrial, while insufficient-evidence reversals bar it. Coupled with the implied-acquittal principle, it defines the outer boundary of the successful-appeal exception.
