The Right to Appeal

There is no federal constitutional right to an appeal. The Constitution does not require a state to provide appellate review of a criminal conviction at all. But where a state chooses to provide appellate review, the procedures it uses must comport with the guarantees of due process and equal protection. The core principle is that a state may not condition meaningful access to its appellate system on a defendant's ability to pay.

What an Indigent Defendant Is Entitled To

  • Trial Transcript: On a first appeal, an indigent defendant must be furnished a free trial transcript (or an adequate substitute) when a transcript is necessary to a meaningful appeal (Griffin v. Illinois)
  • Appointed Counsel: On a first appeal as of right, an indigent defendant is entitled to appointed counsel at state expense (Douglas v. California)
  • Limits of the Right: These equal-protection guarantees extend only to the first appeal as of right; there is no right to appointed counsel for a discretionary second-tier appeal or for a certiorari petition (Ross v. Moffitt)

The doctrine rests on the intersection of the Due Process and Equal Protection Clauses of the Fourteenth Amendment. Because the wealthy can always purchase a transcript and hire counsel, denying those tools to the poor would make the availability of appellate review turn on wealth, an "invidious discrimination" the Constitution forbids.

A defendant is convicted of a felony in state court and wishes to appeal. She cannot afford a lawyer or the cost of the trial transcript. Because the state provides an appeal as of right, it must appoint counsel to represent her and furnish the transcript free of charge; it may not force her to navigate her first appeal alone and blind simply because she is poor.

Harmless Error

Not every error at trial requires reversal of a conviction. An appellate court applies a harmless-error analysis to determine whether a proven error was consequential enough to warrant undoing the verdict. The standard the court applies depends critically on whether the error was of constitutional or merely nonconstitutional dimension.

The Two Harmless-Error Standards

  • Nonconstitutional Error: An appellate court will not reverse for a nonconstitutional trial error unless the error likely affected the outcome of the trial. The burden and threshold are comparatively forgiving to the government.
  • Constitutional Error: A constitutional trial error requires reversal unless the government proves that the error was harmless beyond a reasonable doubt (Chapman v. California). The burden rests on the beneficiary of the error, the prosecution.

The Chapman standard is demanding. It is not enough that the reviewing court believes the defendant probably would have been convicted anyway; the government must show that there is no reasonable possibility that the error contributed to the verdict. Only "trial errors" (errors in the presentation of evidence to the jury) are eligible for this harmless-error review. Structural defects, discussed below, are not.

A prosecutor comments on the defendant's failure to testify, violating the Fifth Amendment privilege against self-incrimination. On appeal, the state concedes the constitutional error but argues that the untainted evidence of guilt was overwhelming. The conviction will be affirmed only if the state carries its burden of proving beyond a reasonable doubt that the improper comment did not contribute to the verdict.
Chapman v. California
386 U.S. 18 (1967)

Before a federal constitutional error can be held harmless, the court must be able to declare a belief that it was harmless beyond a reasonable doubt; the burden of proving harmlessness rests on the beneficiary of the error, the government.

Facts

Chapman and a codefendant were tried for murder in a California court. At the time, a state constitutional provision permitted the prosecutor and the judge to comment on a defendant's failure to testify, and here the prosecutor did so extensively. While the case was on appeal, the Supreme Court decided Griffin v. California, holding such comment unconstitutional. The California Supreme Court found the comment to be error but affirmed the convictions under a state harmless-error rule.

Reasoning

The Court held that whether a federal constitutional error can ever be treated as harmless is itself a question of federal law, and it rejected the notion that all constitutional errors automatically require reversal. Some constitutional errors may be so unimportant in the setting of a particular case that they are harmless. But because the right at stake is constitutional, the Court adopted a stringent standard: the beneficiary of the error, ordinarily the prosecution, must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the error complained of did not contribute to the verdict obtained. Applying that standard, the Court found the pervasive comment on the defendants' silence was not harmless beyond a reasonable doubt and reversed. The Court distinguished this from a mere state law "miscarriage of justice" inquiry, insisting on a federal constitutional test.

Chapman supplies the governing standard for constitutional trial errors: reversal is required unless the government proves the error harmless beyond a reasonable doubt. It anchors the modern distinction between reviewable trial errors and automatically reversible structural defects.

Structural Error

A narrow category of constitutional errors is not subject to harmless-error analysis at all. These "structural errors" are defects in the constitution of the trial mechanism itself, affecting the framework within which the trial proceeds rather than simply an error in the trial process. Because such defects defy analysis by harmless-error standards and infect the entire trial, they require automatic reversal without any inquiry into prejudice.

Recognized Structural Errors (Automatic Reversal)

  • Complete denial of counsel at trial
  • A biased trial judge
  • Racial discrimination in the selection of the grand or petit jury
  • Denial of the right to self-representation
  • Denial of the right to a public trial
  • A constitutionally defective reasonable-doubt jury instruction (Sullivan v. Louisiana)

The distinction was crystallized in Arizona v. Fulminante, where the Court separated "trial errors" (which occur during the presentation of the case to the jury and may be quantitatively assessed against the other evidence to determine harmlessness) from "structural defects" in the trial mechanism (which are not amenable to such assessment). The admission of a coerced confession, the Court held, is a trial error subject to Chapman harmless-error review, not a structural defect.

Why Structural Errors Escape Harmless-Error Review

A defective reasonable-doubt instruction is the paradigm. As Sullivan v. Louisiana explained, when the jury is misinstructed on the reasonable-doubt standard, there has been no jury verdict of guilt beyond a reasonable doubt at all. Harmless-error review asks whether the guilty verdict actually rendered was surely unattributable to the error; but where the error vitiates the verdict itself, there is nothing to review, and reversal is automatic.

A defendant is tried before a judge who has an undisclosed financial stake in the outcome, or is denied any lawyer whatsoever, or is barred from representing himself after a valid Faretta waiver. In each case the reviewing court does not weigh the strength of the evidence; the structural nature of the defect requires reversal regardless of how overwhelming the proof of guilt may appear.

Sufficiency of the Evidence

A defendant may challenge a conviction on the ground that the evidence was legally insufficient to support the verdict. This is a due process claim: because the Due Process Clause requires proof beyond a reasonable doubt of every element of the offense, a conviction resting on constitutionally inadequate evidence cannot stand. But the standard of review is highly deferential to the verdict.

The Jackson v. Virginia Standard

A conviction is affirmed if, viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the prosecution, any rational trier of fact could have found the essential elements of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt. The reviewing court does not ask whether it is itself convinced of guilt, nor does it reweigh the evidence or reassess witness credibility. It asks only whether a rational factfinder could have reached the verdict.

Because the evidence is viewed in the light most favorable to the prosecution, all reasonable inferences are drawn in the government's favor and conflicts in the testimony are resolved in support of the verdict. Reversal for insufficiency is therefore rare and is reserved for cases in which no rational jury could have convicted.

A defendant convicted of possession with intent to distribute argues on appeal that the evidence showed only personal-use quantities. The appellate court will not substitute its judgment for the jury's. If, crediting the government's evidence and drawing every reasonable inference in its favor, a rational juror could have found the intent-to-distribute element beyond a reasonable doubt, the conviction stands even if the court might have decided the case differently.
Jackson v. Virginia
443 U.S. 307 (1979)

On review of the sufficiency of the evidence, the question is whether, after viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the prosecution, any rational trier of fact could have found the essential elements of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt.

Facts

Jackson was convicted of first-degree murder in a Virginia bench trial. He later sought federal habeas corpus relief, contending that the evidence, particularly on the element of premeditation, was constitutionally insufficient to support the conviction. The lower federal courts, applying an older and less protective standard that asked merely whether there was any evidence to support the conviction, denied relief.

Reasoning

The Court held that the earlier "no evidence" rule of Thompson v. Louisville was inadequate to protect the due process guarantee announced in In re Winship that no person may be convicted except upon proof beyond a reasonable doubt of every element of the offense. A record devoid of any evidence is not the only way a conviction can violate due process; a conviction based on evidence from which no rational factfinder could find guilt beyond a reasonable doubt is equally infirm. The Court therefore articulated the controlling standard: a reviewing court, viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the prosecution, asks whether any rational trier of fact could have found the essential elements beyond a reasonable doubt. This standard preserves the factfinder's province to resolve conflicts, weigh evidence, and draw reasonable inferences while ensuring that the constitutional minimum of proof has been met. Applying it, the Court found the evidence of premeditation sufficient and affirmed.

Jackson established the constitutional benchmark for sufficiency review, embedding the reasonable-doubt requirement into appellate and habeas review while remaining deeply deferential to the trier of fact's verdict.

Judge Fact-Finding and Preservation of Error

The standard of review changes with the nature of the ruling challenged. Findings of fact, especially those made by a judge sitting as the trier of fact or when imposing sentence, receive substantial deference, and errors not properly raised below are reviewed only under a stringent plain-error standard.

Clearly Erroneous Review of Judge Fact-Finding

Where the judge is the trier of fact, or imposes sentence based on factual findings, the reviewing court will not disturb those findings unless they are unsupported by substantial, credible evidence in the record. This "clearly erroneous" standard reflects the trial judge's superior vantage point in assessing witness credibility and the evidence firsthand. The appellate court does not retry the facts; it asks only whether the record contains substantial credible support for what the judge found.

Preservation and Plain Error

To obtain ordinary appellate review of a trial error, the defendant generally must have preserved the issue by making a timely objection at trial. Unpreserved errors are reviewed only for "plain error." Plain-error review is far more demanding than review of preserved claims: the error must be clear or obvious, and it must affect substantial rights, meaning it affected the outcome of the proceedings. Even then, a court will typically correct the error only if it seriously affects the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the proceedings.

Defense counsel fails to object when the prosecutor introduces improper evidence. On appeal, the defendant raises the issue for the first time. Because the error was not preserved, the appellate court reviews only for plain error, and it will reverse only if the error was clear, affected the defendant's substantial rights, and seriously undermined the fairness or integrity of the trial, a substantially higher bar than the harmless-error standard applied to preserved claims.
