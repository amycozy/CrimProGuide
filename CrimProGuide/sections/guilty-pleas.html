Overview of Guilty Pleas

The overwhelming majority of criminal convictions in the United States are obtained not through trial but through guilty pleas, most of them the product of plea bargaining. A guilty plea is not merely an admission of past conduct; it is the defendant's consent that judgment of conviction may be entered without a trial, and it simultaneously waives several fundamental constitutional rights. Because so much is surrendered, the Constitution requires that a guilty plea be voluntary, knowing, and intelligent, and that the record affirmatively show as much (Boykin v. Alabama, 395 U.S. 238 (1969)).

Core Requirements of a Valid Plea

  • Voluntary: The plea must not be induced by threats, improper promises, or coercion; it must be the defendant's free choice
  • Knowing and Intelligent: The defendant must understand what the plea entails and what it gives up
  • Affirmative Record: The trial court cannot presume waiver from a silent record; the record must affirmatively demonstrate that the plea meets constitutional standards (Boykin)
  • Factual Basis: Courts ordinarily require a factual basis for the plea to ensure the defendant is pleading guilty to conduct that actually constitutes the offense

Before accepting a plea, the judge conducts a colloquy with the defendant, addressing the defendant personally in open court to confirm understanding of the charges, the rights being waived, and the consequences of the plea. This section walks through each of those requirements, the reach of the effective-assistance guarantee in plea bargaining, the enforceability of the government's promises, and the special forms a plea may take.

Voluntary, Knowing, and Intelligent

Because a guilty plea waives fundamental rights, due process demands that the waiver be made competently and with sufficient awareness of the relevant circumstances. In Boykin v. Alabama, the Supreme Court held that a trial judge may not accept a guilty plea from a silent record; the record must affirmatively show that the plea was intelligent and voluntary. The Court identified three federal constitutional rights that a guilty plea necessarily waives, each of which the defendant must understand he is relinquishing.

Constitutional Rights Waived by a Guilty Plea

  • Right to a Jury Trial: The defendant gives up the Sixth Amendment right to have guilt determined by a jury
  • Right to Confront Accusers: The defendant gives up the Sixth Amendment right to confront and cross-examine the witnesses against him
  • Privilege Against Compelled Self-Incrimination: The defendant gives up the Fifth Amendment privilege by admitting guilt in open court

What the Court Must Ensure the Defendant Understands

  • Nature of the Charge(s): The defendant must understand the offense to which the plea is entered, including its essential elements
  • Rights Being Waived: The jury-trial right, the confrontation right, and the privilege against self-incrimination
  • Consequences of the Plea: The direct consequences, including the maximum authorized sentence and any mandatory minimum sentence
A judge conducting a plea colloquy addresses the defendant personally: "Do you understand that by pleading guilty you give up your right to a trial by jury, your right to confront and cross-examine the witnesses against you, and your right to remain silent? Do you understand that the charge carries a maximum of twenty years and a mandatory minimum of five years?" Affirmative answers on the record help establish that the plea is knowing, intelligent, and voluntary as Boykin requires.
Boykin v. Alabama
395 U.S. 238 (1969)

A guilty plea cannot stand unless the record affirmatively shows that the plea was voluntary, knowing, and intelligent; a court may not presume a valid waiver of the rights surrendered by a guilty plea from a silent record.

Facts

Boykin pleaded guilty to five counts of common-law robbery in an Alabama court. The trial judge accepted the pleas without addressing Boykin personally and without making any inquiry into whether the pleas were voluntary or whether Boykin understood the nature of the charges and the consequences. Boykin was sentenced to death, and nothing in the record indicated that he had knowingly and voluntarily waived his rights.

Reasoning

The Supreme Court reversed, holding that it was reversible error for the trial judge to accept the guilty plea without an affirmative showing on the record that it was intelligent and voluntary. The Court reasoned that a guilty plea is more than a confession; it is itself a conviction that waives several federal constitutional rights, namely the privilege against compelled self-incrimination, the right to trial by jury, and the right to confront one's accusers. Because these rights are of fundamental importance, waiver cannot be presumed from a silent record. The same standard the Court had applied to waiver of the right to counsel in Carnley v. Cochran and Johnson v. Zerbst governs the waiver accomplished by a guilty plea. Requiring the record to show voluntariness and understanding also promotes the reliability of the plea and eases later review by forestalling collateral proceedings over a cold and inadequate record.

Boykin is the foundational modern case on guilty pleas, establishing that the trial court must create an affirmative record showing that the defendant understood the rights waived and entered the plea voluntarily and intelligently. It underlies the plea colloquy required in every American criminal court today.

Direct vs. Collateral Consequences

For a plea to be knowing and intelligent, the defendant must understand the direct consequences of pleading guilty, but generally need not be advised of every collateral consequence. Direct consequences are those with a definite, immediate, and largely automatic effect on the range of the defendant's punishment, such as the maximum authorized sentence and any mandatory minimum. Collateral consequences are effects that flow from the conviction but lie outside the sentencing court's control, such as loss of a professional license, ineligibility for certain benefits, or the possibility of enhanced punishment for a future offense.

The Deportation Exception (Padilla)

  • General Rule: The court's plea colloquy need not cover collateral consequences for the plea to be constitutionally valid
  • Sixth Amendment Duty of Counsel: Even where the court need not advise, defense counsel has an independent obligation to give competent advice about consequences that are truly enmeshed with the criminal case
  • Deportation: Because deportation is a particularly severe and, for many offenses, virtually automatic consequence of conviction, counsel must advise a noncitizen defendant that a guilty plea carries a risk of deportation (Padilla v. Kentucky, 559 U.S. 356 (2010))

Padilla illustrates that the line between direct and collateral consequences governs what the court must explain in the colloquy, but it does not exhaust what the Sixth Amendment requires of defense counsel. A plea can be knowing as to the court's colloquy yet still be attacked as the product of ineffective assistance if counsel affirmatively misadvised, or failed to advise, the defendant about deportation.

Padilla v. Kentucky
559 U.S. 356 (2010)

The Sixth Amendment requires defense counsel to advise a noncitizen client that pleading guilty to a crime carries a risk of deportation; the failure to give such advice, or the giving of affirmatively wrong advice, can constitute constitutionally deficient performance.

Facts

Jose Padilla, a lawful permanent resident of the United States for over forty years and a Vietnam War veteran, was charged in Kentucky with transporting a large amount of marijuana. His attorney told him that he "did not have to worry about immigration status since he had been in the country so long." Relying on that advice, Padilla pleaded guilty. In fact, his conviction made his deportation virtually mandatory. He sought postconviction relief, arguing that counsel's incorrect advice constituted ineffective assistance.

Reasoning

The Supreme Court held that constitutionally competent counsel would have advised Padilla that his conviction made him subject to automatic deportation. Rather than resting on the direct-versus-collateral distinction, the Court found that distinction ill suited to evaluating a claim about counsel's performance, because deportation is a uniquely severe penalty intimately tied to the criminal process. Applying the performance prong of Strickland v. Washington, the Court reasoned that where the deportation consequence is truly clear, as it was under the immigration statute here, counsel's duty to give correct advice is equally clear; where the law is not succinct and straightforward, counsel need only advise that the plea may carry a risk of adverse immigration consequences. The Court declined to limit its holding to affirmative misadvice, concluding that silence is no defense when the stakes are so high. The Court remanded for a determination of prejudice under Strickland's second prong.

Padilla extended the effective-assistance guarantee beyond the trial process into the advice that induces a plea, and it recognized deportation as a consequence so serious that counsel must address it even though it is technically collateral. It is a leading modern example of ineffective assistance in the plea context.

Effective Assistance of Counsel in Plea Bargaining

Because the negotiation of a plea is a critical stage of the prosecution, the Sixth Amendment right to the effective assistance of counsel applies with full force to plea bargaining. The familiar two-part test of Strickland v. Washington governs: the defendant must show that counsel's performance was deficient and that the deficiency caused prejudice. In the plea context, the Supreme Court has given concrete content to both prongs.

Counsel's Duties During Plea Bargaining

  • Communicate Formal Offers: Counsel must convey to the defendant formal plea offers from the prosecution on terms that may be favorable; letting a favorable offer lapse without informing the defendant is deficient performance (Missouri v. Frye)
  • Give Competent Advice: Counsel must not give advice so incorrect that it causes the defendant to reject a favorable offer and stand trial, resulting in a worse outcome (Lafler v. Cooper)
  • Advise on Deportation: Counsel must warn a noncitizen defendant of the deportation risk of a plea (Padilla v. Kentucky)

Prejudice When a Favorable Plea Is Lost

  • Rejected or Lapsed Offer: To show prejudice, the defendant must demonstrate a reasonable probability that, but for counsel's errors, he would have accepted the plea, that the prosecution would not have withdrawn it, and that the court would have accepted its terms
  • Worse Outcome Required: The result of the plea process must have been less favorable to the defendant than the offer he lost, whether a longer sentence or conviction on more serious charges
  • Remedy: Courts may order the offer reoffered, or may resentence, exercising discretion to neutralize the constitutional violation (Lafler)
A prosecutor offers a plea to a reduced charge carrying a recommended sentence of four to seven years, set to expire in one week. Defense counsel never tells the defendant about the offer, and it lapses. The defendant later pleads guilty to the original charge and receives a longer sentence. Under Missouri v. Frye, counsel's failure to communicate the offer is deficient performance, and the defendant may obtain relief if he can show the offer would have been accepted and the outcome would have been more favorable.

Enforcing the Plea Agreement

A plea agreement is, in effect, a bargain: the defendant surrenders the right to trial in exchange for the government's promises. When the defendant's guilty plea rests in any significant degree on a promise by the prosecutor, that promise must be fulfilled. In Santobello v. New York, the Supreme Court held that the government's breach of a plea agreement entitles the defendant to a remedy, even where the breach was inadvertent and even where the breach may not have influenced the sentencing judge.

Breach and Remedy

  • Promises Must Be Kept: If the inducement for the plea is a prosecutorial promise (for example, to recommend a particular sentence or to make no recommendation), the government must honor it
  • Inadvertence Is No Excuse: A breach by a different prosecutor who was unaware of the promise still violates the agreement
  • Remedy: The defendant is entitled to relief, which may be specific performance of the agreement before a different judge, or withdrawal of the plea; the choice of remedy is generally for the court on remand

The same principle of mutual obligation means the defendant, too, must abide by the terms of the bargain; a defendant who breaches may forfeit the benefits promised in exchange. But because the government holds superior bargaining power and drafts the agreement, courts construe ambiguities against the prosecution and hold it strictly to its word.

Alford and Nolo Contendere Pleas

Ordinarily a defendant who pleads guilty admits the acts charged. But a plea may be valid even where the defendant refuses to admit guilt, so long as the plea is voluntary and there is a strong factual basis for it. In North Carolina v. Alford, the Supreme Court upheld a guilty plea entered by a defendant who simultaneously protested his innocence, reasoning that an individual accused of a crime may voluntarily and knowingly consent to the imposition of a sentence even if he is unwilling or unable to admit his participation in the acts constituting the crime.

The Alford Plea

  • Innocence Maintained: The defendant pleads guilty while asserting that he did not commit the offense, typically to obtain the benefit of a bargain and avoid a harsher outcome at trial
  • Voluntary and Intelligent: As with any plea, the record must show the plea was a voluntary and intelligent choice among the alternatives available
  • Strong Factual Basis: Because the defendant does not admit guilt, the court must find a strong factual basis in the record establishing that the defendant actually committed the crime

The Nolo Contendere Plea

  • No Contest: A plea of nolo contendere ("I will not contest it") permits conviction and sentencing without an admission of guilt
  • Collateral Effect: Unlike a guilty plea, a nolo plea generally cannot be used against the defendant as an admission in a later civil suit arising from the same conduct
  • Court's Consent: A defendant ordinarily has no absolute right to plead nolo contendere; acceptance rests in the court's discretion
A defendant charged with first-degree murder, facing the death penalty, is offered a plea to second-degree murder. Believing he is innocent but recognizing that the evidence against him is strong, he pleads guilty to second-degree murder to avoid the risk of execution while telling the court he did not commit the killing. Under North Carolina v. Alford, the court may accept this plea if the record contains a strong factual basis for guilt and the plea is voluntary and intelligent.

Withdrawal and Waiver

A defendant may seek to withdraw a plea that fails to meet the constitutional standard. A plea that was not voluntary, knowing, and intelligent, whether because of an inadequate colloquy, coercion, ineffective assistance, or a broken government promise, is subject to withdrawal or later collateral attack. Conversely, a plea that satisfies those standards has powerful preclusive force.

Grounds for Withdrawal

  • Involuntary or Unknowing Plea: A plea that does not meet the Boykin standard may be withdrawn
  • Ineffective Assistance: A plea induced by constitutionally deficient advice (for example, a Padilla violation) may be vacated on a showing of prejudice
  • Government Breach: A prosecutorial breach of the plea agreement supports withdrawal or specific performance (Santobello)

What a Valid Plea Waives

  • Nonjurisdictional Defects: A voluntary and intelligent guilty plea waives most nonjurisdictional defects in the proceedings that occurred before the plea, including many claims of constitutional violation in the acquisition of evidence (Tollett v. Henderson)
  • Antecedent Constitutional Claims: After a valid plea, the defendant generally may not raise independent claims relating to the deprivation of constitutional rights that occurred prior to the plea; the focus shifts to whether the plea itself was intelligent and voluntary and whether counsel's advice fell within the range of competence
  • What Survives: Certain claims survive a guilty plea, notably challenges to the court's jurisdiction and claims that the charge is one the State may not constitutionally prosecute at all

Under Tollett v. Henderson, when a defendant pleads guilty on the advice of counsel, he may attack the voluntary and intelligent character of the plea by showing that counsel's advice was constitutionally inadequate, but he may not thereafter resurrect antecedent constitutional deprivations as independent grounds for relief. The guilty plea thus represents a break in the chain of events that preceded it, and the validity of the plea itself becomes the central inquiry.
