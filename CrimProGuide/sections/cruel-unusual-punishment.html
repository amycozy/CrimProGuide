Overview of the Eighth Amendment

The Eighth Amendment provides that "cruel and unusual punishments" shall not be inflicted. The Cruel and Unusual Punishments Clause bars two distinct categories of penalties: (1) inherently barbaric or torturous punishments, and (2) punishments that are grossly disproportionate to the crime for which they are imposed. The Clause applies to the states through the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment.

The Two Core Prohibitions

  • Barbaric Punishments: Methods of punishment that are inherently cruel, torturous, or degrading are forbidden regardless of the crime
  • Grossly Disproportionate Punishments: A sentence that is grossly disproportionate to the gravity of the offense violates the Eighth Amendment
  • Evolving Standards of Decency: The Court measures what is "cruel and unusual" against "the evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society," looking to objective indicia such as legislative enactments and jury practice

Two analytical tracks flow from the Clause. For terms of imprisonment, the Court applies a narrow, highly deferential proportionality review and rarely strikes down a sentence. For the death penalty, by contrast, the Court applies far more searching scrutiny, imposing procedural requirements and categorical limits on who may be executed and for which crimes.

Proportionality and Terms of Imprisonment

The Eighth Amendment contains a "narrow proportionality principle" that applies to noncapital sentences. A punishment is unconstitutional only if it is grossly disproportionate to the crime. The leading framework comes from Solem v. Helm, which identifies three factors courts use to assess proportionality.

The Solem Proportionality Factors

  1. Gravity of the Offense vs. Harshness of the Penalty: How serious is the crime compared to the severity of the punishment imposed?
  2. Intra-Jurisdiction Comparison: The sentences imposed on other criminals for other crimes in the same jurisdiction
  3. Inter-Jurisdiction Comparison: The sentences imposed for the same crime in other jurisdictions

In practice the Court is highly deferential to legislative sentencing judgments for terms of imprisonment. A reviewing court reaches the comparative second and third Solem factors only after a threshold inference of gross disproportionality arises from comparing the crime to the sentence. That threshold is rarely met, and successful challenges to prison terms are exceedingly rare.

Highly Deferential Review in Practice

  • Harmelin v. Michigan: Upheld a sentence of life without the possibility of parole for possession of a large quantity of cocaine, even as a first offense
  • Ewing v. California: Upheld a sentence of 25 years to life imposed under California's "three-strikes" recidivist law for the theft of golf clubs
  • Rummel v. Estelle: Upheld a life sentence under a recidivist statute for a series of minor property/fraud offenses

A defendant with two prior convictions is sentenced to 25 years to life under a three-strikes law after shoplifting merchandise worth several hundred dollars. Although the triggering offense is minor, the sentence reflects the state's legitimate interest in incapacitating and deterring repeat offenders. Under Ewing, the sentence is not grossly disproportionate and does not violate the Eighth Amendment.

Solem v. Helm
463 U.S. 277 (1983)

The Eighth Amendment prohibits sentences that are grossly disproportionate to the crime, and courts assess proportionality using three objective factors.

Facts

Helm was convicted of uttering a "no account" check for $100, ordinarily punishable by a maximum of five years. Because he had six prior nonviolent felony convictions, he was sentenced under South Dakota's recidivist statute to life imprisonment without the possibility of parole.

Reasoning

The Court held that the Eighth Amendment's proportionality principle applies to prison sentences, not just the death penalty. It articulated three guiding factors: (1) the gravity of the offense compared with the harshness of the penalty; (2) the sentences imposed on other criminals in the same jurisdiction; and (3) the sentences imposed for commission of the same crime in other jurisdictions. Applying these factors, the Court found the life-without-parole sentence grossly disproportionate to a series of minor nonviolent offenses, particularly because it was the harshest available and exceeded what other jurisdictions would impose.

Provides the analytical framework for noncapital proportionality challenges, though later decisions such as Harmelin and Ewing confined it to a threshold, highly deferential inquiry rarely resolved in the defendant's favor.

The Death Penalty

Capital punishment is not per se unconstitutional. In Gregg v. Georgia, the Court held that the death penalty may be imposed consistent with the Eighth Amendment when it is administered under a guided-discretion statute that channels and limits the sentencer's discretion, typically through bifurcated proceedings and consideration of aggravating and mitigating factors. This holding followed Furman v. Georgia, which had invalidated then-existing capital schemes for being applied in an arbitrary and capricious manner.

Procedural Requirements for Capital Sentencing

  • Guided Discretion Required: The sentencer's discretion must be guided by clear and objective standards to prevent arbitrary imposition (Gregg v. Georgia)
  • No Mandatory Death Sentences: A statute that automatically imposes death for a given crime, without individualized consideration of the offender and the offense, is unconstitutional (Woodson v. North Carolina)
  • Individualized Consideration of Mitigation: The sentencer must be permitted to consider all relevant mitigating evidence about the defendant's character and record

Death Is Disproportionate for Crimes That Do Not Take a Life

  • Rape of an Adult Woman: The death penalty is grossly disproportionate and unconstitutional as punishment for the rape of an adult woman (Coker v. Georgia)
  • Rape of a Child: The death penalty may not be imposed for the rape of a child where the crime did not result, and was not intended to result, in the victim's death (Kennedy v. Louisiana)
  • General Principle: Absent a death, the death penalty is a disproportionate penalty for crimes against individuals; the Court reserves capital punishment for the most serious homicides

A state enacts a statute making death the automatic penalty for the murder of a police officer, with no separate sentencing phase and no opportunity to present mitigating evidence. Even though the underlying crime is a homicide for which death may be constitutional, the mandatory nature of the scheme violates the Eighth Amendment under Woodson because it denies individualized consideration of the offender and offense.

Kennedy v. Louisiana
554 U.S. 407 (2008)

The Eighth Amendment bars imposition of the death penalty for the rape of a child where the crime did not result in, and was not intended to result in, the victim's death.

Facts

Kennedy was convicted under a Louisiana statute that authorized capital punishment for the rape of a child under twelve. The victim, his stepdaughter, survived the assault. Kennedy was sentenced to death, and the Louisiana Supreme Court affirmed.

Reasoning

Applying the evolving standards of decency, the Court surveyed objective indicia of a national consensus and found that a large majority of jurisdictions did not authorize death for child rape. Exercising its own independent judgment, the Court reasoned that the death penalty must be reserved for the most serious crimes and, in crimes against individuals, for those that take the life of the victim. Because the crime, however grievous, did not result in death, capital punishment was a grossly disproportionate penalty. The Court also expressed concern about the reliability and consistency of applying death for nonhomicide crimes.

Extends Coker v. Georgia beyond adult rape and establishes a categorical rule that, for crimes against individuals, the death penalty is limited to offenses that result in the victim's death.

Categorical Exemptions from Execution

Even where the crime is a death-eligible homicide, certain categories of offenders may not be executed at all because doing so would be cruel and unusual. These are status-based, categorical bars keyed to the characteristics of the defendant.

Offenders Who May Not Be Executed

  • The Intellectually Disabled: Execution of an intellectually disabled offender violates the Eighth Amendment because such offenders have diminished culpability and the penological purposes of retribution and deterrence are not adequately served (Atkins v. Virginia)
  • The Legally Insane: A prisoner who is insane at the time of the scheduled execution—who lacks a rational understanding of the reason for and reality of the punishment—may not be executed (Ford v. Wainwright)

A defendant convicted of capital murder presents clinical evidence establishing significant intellectual disability that manifested before adulthood. Regardless of the aggravating circumstances of the crime, the Eighth Amendment categorically bars his execution under Atkins v. Virginia; the appropriate sentence is a term of imprisonment rather than death.

Juvenile Offenders

The Eighth Amendment imposes special limits on punishing offenders who were juveniles—under 18—at the time they committed their crimes. These limits rest on the recognition that juveniles have diminished culpability and greater capacity for change: they are less mature, more susceptible to negative influences, and their characters are not yet fully formed. The relevant age is the offender's age at the time of the crime, not at trial or sentencing.

Progression of Juvenile Sentencing Limits

  • No Execution of Juveniles: The death penalty may not be imposed on an offender who was under 18 at the time of the crime (Roper v. Simmons)
  • No LWOP for Juvenile Nonhomicide Crimes: A juvenile who did not commit homicide may not be sentenced to life without the possibility of parole; the state must give some meaningful opportunity for release based on demonstrated maturity and rehabilitation (Graham v. Florida)
  • No Mandatory LWOP for Juveniles: Even for homicide, a sentencing scheme that mandates life without parole for a juvenile is unconstitutional; the sentencer must be able to consider the offender's youth and its attendant characteristics before imposing that penalty (Miller v. Alabama)
  • Retroactivity: The rule of Miller applies retroactively to cases on collateral review (Montgomery v. Louisiana)

A 17-year-old is convicted of felony murder under a statute that requires an automatic sentence of life without parole for all murder convictions. Because the scheme forecloses any consideration of the defendant's youth, immaturity, and capacity for rehabilitation, it violates the Eighth Amendment under Miller v. Alabama. Under Montgomery, a defendant already serving such a mandatory sentence may seek relief even after his conviction became final.

Roper v. Simmons
543 U.S. 551 (2005)

The Eighth and Fourteenth Amendments forbid imposition of the death penalty on offenders who were under 18 years of age at the time of their crimes.

Facts

Simmons, at age 17, planned and committed a brutal murder, later confessing and reenacting the crime. He was tried as an adult, convicted, and sentenced to death. After the Court held in Atkins that the intellectually disabled could not be executed, Simmons challenged his death sentence on the ground that executing juvenile offenders was likewise unconstitutional.

Reasoning

Consulting the evolving standards of decency, the Court found objective evidence of a national consensus against executing juveniles in the number of states rejecting the practice and the infrequency of its use where permitted. Exercising independent judgment, the Court reasoned that juveniles categorically have diminished culpability: they lack maturity and have an underdeveloped sense of responsibility, they are more vulnerable to negative influences and peer pressure, and their characters are more transitory and less fixed than an adult's. These differences mean the penological justifications of retribution and deterrence apply with lesser force, so death is a disproportionate punishment for juveniles. The Court drew a categorical line at 18, the age society otherwise draws between childhood and adulthood.

Establishes a bright-line categorical bar on the juvenile death penalty and supplies the diminished-culpability rationale later extended to juvenile life-without-parole sentences in Graham and Miller.
